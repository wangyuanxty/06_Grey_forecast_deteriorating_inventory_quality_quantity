## Highlights

- A grey forecasting modelling approach is proposed for establishing inventory models of deteriorating products. By applying the grey forecasting approach to identify inventory differential equations, this study develops a new framework for constructing inventory models directly from inventory data.
- A special inventory system considering interdependent quality and quantity decay is established. The quality level is defined as the proportion of unspoiled items, while the demand rate is modeled as a multiplicative function of both quality level and price.
- A grey forecasting modelling approach for deteriorating inventory is proposed. By applying the accumulated generating operator to uncover unobserved information, the fitting problem of the inventory differential equation is transformed into a regression problem.


#### Abstract

To address the challenge of establishing inventory models for deteriorating products under uncertain environments, this paper first proposes a grey forecasting approach to construct an inventory model based on historical inventory data. We consider an inventory system for deteriorating products with interdependent quantity and quality decay, and formulate a differential equation to characterize the dynamic evolution of inventory levels. With the support of cumulative generation operator, the identification of this differential equation is transformed as a joint regression problem. Using the identified equation, an optimization model is developed to derive inventory decisions. A simulation example is performed to validate the modelling framework, with results confirming high accuracy in parameter estimation. Overall, the study highlights the potential of grey forecasting models to enable prediction and optimization in practical inventory management.

## 1. Introduction

With the ongoing upgrading of dietary patterns in China, consumer demand for fresh agricultural products has shifted from basic availability to quality-oriented consumption. However, substantial losses of these products still occur throughout the circulation process. Across the entire supply chain, total loss rates reach $7.9 \%$ for staple grains, $7.7 \%$ for vegetables, and $13.2 \%$ for fruits (Lu et al., 2022). Grain losses occur primarily during the production stage, whereas vegetable and fruit losses are concentrated in postharvest handling and retail stages. As highly perishable goods, fresh agricultural products impose stricter demands on inventory management within the supply chain. Serving as a critical link between ordering and sales, inventory management not only affects direct spoilage losses but also contributes to demand loss caused by declining freshness.

For deteriorating products like fresh agricultural foods, classical inventory models first formulate a differential equation to describe inventory level, and then develop an inventory optimization model to derive inventory decisions. However, because deterioration processes and demand patterns are inherently uncertain, specifying the inventory differential equation in realworld scenarios is highly challenging. Consequently, a central scientific question is how to leverage historical data to reveal the inventory differential equation and, in turn, support the construction of inventory optimization models. Given that grey forecasting models are well suited for identifying differential equations from observational data, this article introduces a grey forecasting approach to discover the inventory differential equation and subsequently integrates the identified equation into inventory optimization models. The following section reviews theoretical advancements on inventory models of deteriorating products and grey forecasting models, and then outlines the research motivation and overall framework of this study.

### 1.1. Inventory model of deteriorating products

Deteriorating products refer to items whose quality, performance, or value are significantly diminished or even lost due to the influence of physical, chemical, or biological factors. According to the standardized classification, the deteriorating products are categorized into three distinct types: (i) deteriorating products with random lifespans, (ii) deteriorating products with fixed lifespans (often referred to as perishable products), and (iii) obsolete products (Perez \& Torres, 2020). Deteriorating products with random lifespans are those whose lifespan can be treated as a random variable. This class encompasses the majority of fresh foods, including fruits, vegetables, and meats. In contrast, deteriorating products with fixed lifespans are characterized by a definitive expiration date. Typical examples include packaged foods, canned foods, batteries, photographic film, and blood products. The obsolete products, on the other hand, do not lose value but instead depreciate over time due to factors such as changing consumer preferences or technological advances. Representative examples include fashion items and digital products.

For deteriorating products with random lifespans, Ghare \& Schrader (1963) introduced a pioneering inventory model, namely the economic order quantity (EOQ) model. They employed a differential equation to describe the dynamics of inventory levels and subsequently established the EOQ model to guide replenishment decisions. The deterioration rate and demand rate are two driving factors of deteriorating inventory, which have attracted significant attention from many scholars. In early studies, most deterioration rates were often assumed to be constant, such as Balkhi \& Benkherouf (2004); Chang (2004), implying that product lifespans follow an exponential distribution. Later research recognized that deterioration rates can vary over time, giving rise to the concept of time-varying deterioration rates. The two-parameter Weibull distribution (Wee, 1999), the three-parameter Weibull distribution (Chakrabarty, Giri, \& Chaudhuri, 1998), and the Gamma distribution (Tadikamalla, 1978) have all been employed to design time-varying deterioration rates. Moreover, some scholars have defined deterioration rates using the timeproportional function (Mukhopadhyay, Mukherjee, \& Chaudhuri, 2004), the time-linear function (Dye \& Hsieh, 2012), and the time-inverse proportional function (Balkhi, 2001).

The aforementioned deterioration rates are essentially quantitative deterioration rates, as they describe the patterns of inventory quantity loss. However, in addition to causing direct quantitative losses, the deterioration process also results in qualitative losses, manifesting in diminished appearance, texture, and nutritional quality. As a result, several scholars have integrated quality decay or freshness decay into inventory model. Bai \& Kendall (2008) defined the freshness level as an exponentially decreasing function, which aligns with the exponential decay law described in prior research. Maihami \& Nakhai Kamalabadi (2012) expressed the demand rate as the product of a linear price term and a negative exponential time term, where the latter proxies quality level. Piramuthu \& Zhou (2013) assumed that product life adheres to an exponential distribution and derived an exponentially decreasing quality level. Qin, Wang, \& Wei (2014) and Rabbani, Pourmohammad Zia, \& Rafiei (2016) jointly account for both quantity depletion and quality deterioration of deteriorating products, describing the latter through a differential equation. Liu, Zhang, \& Tang (2015) formulated the quality of perishable products using a differential equation. Banerjee \&

Agrawal (2017) demonstrated that the freshness will decay after a threshold period, and defined a non-instantaneous decay function.

The demand rate is another driving factor of deteriorating inventory. Qin et al. (2014) posit that the demand rate is influenced by quality, price, and displayed inventory, and employ a multiplicative formulation to integrate the quality and price effects. Liu et al. (2015) adopted a multiplicative approach to integrate the price and quality effects, introducing an additional demand coefficient compared to other studies. Rabbani et al. (2016) further defined the demand rate as a complex function of price, price variation, and quality level. Banerjee \& Agrawal (2017) defined a demand function of price and freshness, and integrated these two factors through multiplication. Feng, Chan, \& Cárdenas-Barrón (2017) expressed demand as a multiplicative function of price effect, freshness effect, and inventory effect. Xu, Zhou, Zhao, Shi, \& Bingzhen (2022) assumed a linear relationship between the price effect and the freshness effect, proposed an optimal pricing model for non-immediately deteriorating products.

In traditional inventory models, the deterioration rate and demand rate are combined to form inventory differential equations, which are subsequently incorporated into an optimization model to generate inventory decisions. However, in the real world, the deterioration characteristic and demand function are usually not known exactly or too intricate to work with Keskin, Li, \& Song (2022). For instance, if demand is considered to depend on price, the price sensitivity coefficient is difficult to determine. Due to incomplete product information, theoretical inventory models are difficult to apply in practice, creating a gap between the theory and application of deteriorating inventory. In recent years, data-driven inventory management has emerged as a key approach to addressing uncertainties in an uncertain environment, which leverages advanced models and algorithms to construct inventory models directly from operational data (Qin, Simchi-Levi, \& Wang, 2022). In the context of deteriorating inventory, this approach involves deriving inventory differential equations from observed data and subsequently developing optimization models.

### 1.2. Grey forecasting model

The grey forecasting model, a fundamental component of grey system theory, constructs differential equations for grey systems. It interprets time series as observations of dynamic systems and employs sequence generation operators to fit differential equations. The grey forecasting principle was first introduced by Deng \& Julong (1989), and the technique of fitting differential equations to time series is subsequently known as grey forecasting modelling. Over the past four decades, various grey forecasting models have been developed to capture system dynamics across a wide range of fields.

Univariate grey forecasting models, which are designed for a single behavioural variable, have been the subject of extensive research. Cui, Liu, Zeng, \& Xie (2013) first constructed the NGM( $1,1, t$ ) model for non-homogeneous exponential trends by modifying the forcing term. Qian, Dang, \& Liu (2012) first introduced the time power term and developed the grey GM( $1,1, t^{\alpha}$ ) model. Dang \& Baolei (2017) investigated the GMP(1,1,N) model to capture both partial exponential and polynomial characteristics. Gatabazi, Mba, Pindza, \& Labuschagne (2019) developed a grey Lotka-Volterra model to capture the interplay between multiple variables. Based on the traditional Richards model, Luo, Duan, \& Xu (2021) applied the differential information principle to propose a grey Richards model. Duan \& Nie (2022) introduced a grey forecasting model derived from the SIR infectious disease model. Building on the Bass differential equation, Fan, Wang, \& Pei (2023) developed an extended grey Bass model by incorporating the dynamic market capacity. Ye, Xie, Boylan, \& Shang (2024) extended the development coefficient to a time-varying function and used Integral matching to estimate model parameters. Pour, Fard, \& Zeng (2025) proposed a grey Von Bertalanffy model to predict energy production and consumption.

The multivariate grey forecasting model extends the univariate framework by incorporating the influence of relevant factors on the system behavior variable. To address the challenges of solving multivariate differential equations, Tien (2012) introduced the convolution integral approach and developed the grey convolution model. Zeng, Li, \& Ma (2020) enriched the multivariate grey model by incorporating a time-trend term, while Zeng \& Tuo (2025) proposed a novel formulation with a time-lag parameter to capture asynchronous correlations. For non-linear extensions, Wang \& Ye (2017) generalized the univariate grey power model to the multivariate case. Ma \& Liu (2018) applied kernel functions to characterize the effects of relevant variables on system behavior, and Zhang, Rao, Xiao, Hu, \& Goh (2024) further advanced the model by integrating linear correction terms and random interference components. Zhou, Zhao, Li, \& Han (2025) proposed a non-linear time-varying grey prediction framework by introducing the time-varying development coefficient.

Research on grey forecasting models has become quite extensive, yet effectively translating theoretical advances into real-world applications remains a significant challenge. While numerous scholars have proposed diverse grey forecasting models through innovations in structure, little attention has been given to the matching relationship between model forms and specific application scenarios. For instance, references (Cui, Liu, Zeng, Xie, 2013, Dang, Baolei, 2017, Qian, Dang, Liu, 2012) constructed several grey extended models by improving the forcing term, but these modifications lack targeted explanations in relation to the research object. Similarly, references (Ye, Xie, Boylan, Shang, 2024, Zhou, Zhao, Li, Han, 2025) extended the development
coefficient to the time-varying functions, but the connection between these coefficients and the research context remains obscure. These limitations highlight the need for a deeper exploration of grey forecasting modelling within specific application domains. Accordingly, this paper seeks to advance the integration of grey forecasting theory with real-world practice, using the example of the inventory problem of deteriorating products.

### 1.3. Motivation

For deteriorating products with random lifespans, data-driven inventory management entails formulating inventory differential equations and constructing optimization models from inventory data. As elaborated in Section 1.2, grey forecasting modelling establishes differential equations by excavating the dynamic characteristics, thereby enabling the understanding, forecasting, and optimization of dynamic processes. The task of discovering differential equations from inventory data aligns closely with the principles of grey forecasting modelling. Accordingly, this study utilizes grey forecasting modelling to extract the underlying differential equations from inventory data and then constructs an inventory optimization model to provide wise inventory decisions.

## 2. Inventory system analysis

This section first analyses the inventory system for deteriorating products subject to quality decay and quantity decay. The quality level and demand rate are successively defined, and a differential equation is formulated to describe the evolution of inventory levels.

### 2.1. Quality level of deteriorating products

In existing literature (Banerjee, Agrawal, 2017, Liu, Zhang, Tang, 2015, Qin, Wang, Wei, 2014, Rabbani, Pourmohammad Zia, Rafiei, 2016), the quantity deterioration process and the quality degradation process are assumed to be independent. In reality, however, the two processes are completely independent. An increase in the number of spoiled units diminishes consumers' perceptions and evaluations of product quality. It is generally recognized that a higher level of spoilage corresponds to a lower perceived quality level. This paper considers the interdependence between quality degradation and quantity decay, and defines quality levels based on the quantity decay characteristics.

Assuming that the product life follows a distribution with probability density function of $f(t)$ and cumulative distribution function of $F(t)$. According to reliability theory, the reliability level is given by $R(t)=1-F(t)$. The deterioration rate can be expressed as $\theta(t)=f(t) / R(t)$. Without considering the impact of demand on the inventory level, the inventory dynamics can be described by the differential equation $\frac{d I(t)}{d t}=-\theta(t) I(t)$, with the initial condition $I\left(t_{0}\right)=Q$. Here, $\theta(t)$ is referred to as the quantity decay rate. At time $t$, the quantities of deteriorated and non-deteriorated products are given by $Q F(t)$ and $Q R(t)$, respectively. $Q F(t)$ represents the number of products that have lost their utility by time $t$, while $Q R(t)$ represents the number of products that still retain their original performance. Based on the effective inventory quantity available for sale, consumers can evaluate the overall quality level of the inventory. Therefore, the greater the number of products that have deteriorated, the smaller the effective stock available for sale, and the lower the average quality level of the inventory. To describe the quality of inventory products, the quality level is defined as the ratio of non-deteriorated products to the initial inventory quantity, expressed by

$$
\begin{equation*}
q(t)=\frac{Q R(t)}{Q}=R(t) \tag{1}
\end{equation*}
$$

As the number of products that lose their utility increases, the quality level $q(t)$ gradually decreases. The decay rate of quality level is $r(t)=f(t) / R(t)$, which implies that the rate of quality degradation is equal to the rate of quantity decay. If the product lifetime follows an exponential distribution with parameter $\lambda$, the expression for the quality level is $q(t)=e^{-\lambda t}$.

### 2.2. Demand rate depends on quality level and price

Product quality and price are widely recognized as pivotal determinants of market demand. Within the quality-demand nexus, Bai \& Kendall (2008) specify demand as the product of inventory level and freshness. Piramuthu \& Zhou (2013) further include allocated shelf space, capturing demand through a polynomial function. Qin et al. (2014) propose a generalized demand function by considering displayed stock, quality level, and selling price. Banerjee \& Agrawal (2017) allow the demand regime to switch: price-only at product launch, then price and freshness once degradation becomes perceptible. Feng et al. (2017) formulate a multiplicative form of freshness, whereas Xu et al. (2022) adopt an additive specification to incorporate freshness and additional covariates. Considering the positive correlation between quality and demand, this study uses quality level to directly represent the quality effect, i.e., $D(q)=q(t)$.

The price-demand nexus has been thoroughly investigated. Maihami \& Nakhai Kamalabadi (2012) model demand as a linear, time-varying function that decreases in price. Liu et al. (2015) character the demand rate as a bivariate function of the retail price and the quality. Rabbani et al. (2016) specify the demand rate as a joint function of the selling price and quality level. Feng et al. (2017) employ an exponential form, postulating demand proportional to $e^{-\lambda p}$. Chang, Cheng, \& Ouyang (2015) adopt a power specification, reflecting the high price elasticity of demand for deteriorating items. Khan, Shaikh, Konstantaras, Bhunia, \& Cárdenas-Barrón (2020) extend the price effect by multiplicatively including advertising frequency. Collectively, the literature converges on three canonical functional forms: the linear decay $D(p)=\alpha-\beta p$ (Khan, Shaikh, Konstantaras, Bhunia, CárdenasBarrón, 2020, Liu, Zhang, Tang, 2015, Maihami, Nakhai Kamalabadi, 2012), the power decay $D(p)=\alpha p^{-\beta}$ (Chang, Cheng, Ouyang, 2015, Qin, Wang, Wei, 2014), and the exponential decay $D e^{-\lambda p}$ (Banerjee, Agrawal, 2017, Feng, Chan, Cárdenas-Barrón, 2017). To preserve analytical tractability while retaining economic interpretability, we adopt the linear decay $D(p)=\alpha-\beta p$ to characterize the dependence between demand and price.

When demand is shaped by multiple factors, a key challenge lies in integrating these effects into a unified model. Most studies, such as Banerjee \& Agrawal (2017); Liu et al. (2015); Qin et al. (2014); Rabbani et al. (2016), adopt a multiplicative function to capture the joint impact of quality and price, while a few, such as Xu et al. (2022), apply an additive (subtractive) form. Compared with linear combinations, multiplicative formulations more effectively reflect the interaction between price and quality in shaping demand. Following this line of research, the present study adopts the multiplicative function to incorporate quality and price effects, leading to the demand rate specification $d(t, p)=D(p) D(q)=(\alpha-\beta p) e^{-\lambda t}$.

### 2.3. Differential equation of inventory levels

Based on the above analysis, the inventory system is established under the following assumptions:

1. The inventory system involves only one item and operates over an infinite planning horizon.
2. The product lifespan follows an independent exponential distribution with parameter $\theta$, thus the quantity decay rate and the quality level are described by $\theta(t)=\lambda$ and $R(t)=e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda>0)$.
3. The price effect $D(p)=\alpha-\beta p$, where $\alpha>0$ is the potential demand and $\beta>0$ is the price sensitivity coefficient.
4. The demand rate is a multiplicative function of the price effect and quality level, namely $d(t, p)=D(p) R(t)=(\alpha-\beta p) e^{-\lambda t}$. If the purchase price is $c$, the selling price $p$ satisfies $c \leqslant p \leqslant \frac{\alpha}{\beta}$.
5. The replenishment rate is infinite and the lead time is zero.
6. Shortage is not allowed.

The operating mechanism of the inventory system is illustrated in Fig. 2. At the beginning of the replenishment cycle, denoted by $t_{0}$, a batch of $Q$ units with an initial quality level of 1 are added to the inventory. Subsequently, the inventory level $I(t)$ begins to decrease from $Q$, while the quality level begins to decay from 1 . Due to demand and deterioration, the inventory level gradually declines to zero, reaching the end of the replenishment cycle at $t_{T}$. Once the inventory is depleted, the system initiates the next cycle by placing a replenishment order, and the inventory is immediately restored.

During the replenishment cycle $\left[t_{0}, t_{T}\right]$, the inventory quantity level $I(t)$ is governed by the differential equation:

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d I(t)}{d t}=-\lambda I(t)-d(t, p) \tag{2}
\end{equation*}
$$

where the boundary conditions $I\left(t_{0}\right)=Q$ and $I\left(t_{T}\right)=0$. The demand function is expressed as

$$
\begin{equation*}
d(t, p)=(\alpha-\beta p) e^{-\lambda\left(t-t_{0}\right)} \tag{3}
\end{equation*}
$$

By solving differential equation (2) with boundary condition $I\left(t_{T}\right)=0$, the inventory quantity level is described by

$$
\begin{equation*}
I(t)=(\alpha-\beta p) \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\left(t-t_{0}\right)}\left[t_{T}-t\right] \tag{4}
\end{equation*}
$$

Let the replenishment cycle be $T=t_{T}-t_{0}$. The initial order quantity can then be obtained from the boundary condition:

$$
\begin{equation*}
I\left(t_{0}\right)=Q=(\alpha-\beta p) T \tag{5}
\end{equation*}
$$

The total demand over one replenishment cycle is given by $Q_{d}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{T}} d(\tau, p) \mathrm{d} \tau=\frac{\alpha-\beta p}{\lambda}\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}\right]$. The total quantity of deteriorated items can be calculated either as $Q_{q}=\lambda \int_{t_{0}}^{t_{T}} I(\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau$ or equivalently as $Q_{q}=Q-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{T}} d(\tau, p) \mathrm{d} \tau$. Thus, we obtain

$$
\begin{equation*}
Q_{q}=(\alpha-\beta p) T-\frac{\alpha-\beta p}{\lambda}\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}\right] \tag{6}
\end{equation*}
$$

This section defines the inventory system for deteriorating products and formulates a differential equation to represent the inventory levels. The structural validity and parametrization of the differential equation are crucial to the effectiveness of inventory models. However, when demand patterns and product deterioration characteristics are uncertain, the equation parameters may be unknown in practice. The following section will introduce a grey forecasting approach for constructing the differential equation based on inventory data.
3. Grey forecasting modelling for deteriorating inventory

This section investigates the parameter estimation problem for the differential equation. Based on the inventory data, the estimation problem is reformulated as a joint regression problem with the aid of the accumulated generating operator.

### 3.1. Problem description

Let the $i$-th order quantity be $Q_{i}$. The ordered products arrive in inventory at time $t_{i 0}$, are sold at a price $p_{i}$, and are depleted by time $t_{i T}$. Denote the inventory level at time $t_{i, j}$ as $I_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)$, and define the inventory change as $\nabla I_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=I_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)-I_{i}\left(t_{i, j-1}\right)$. The observation of inventory change at time $t_{i, j}$ is $l_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=\nabla I_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)+\psi_{i j}$, where $\psi_{i j}$ is the observation error. Then the sequence of inventory changes for the $i$-th replenishment is expressed by $\boldsymbol{l}_{i}=\left[l_{i}\left(t_{i, 1}\right), l_{i}\left(t_{i, 2}\right), \cdots, l_{i}\left(t_{i, n_{i}}\right)\right]^{\top}$. Similarly, the demand observation at time $t_{i, j}$ is $d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=\int_{t_{i, j-1}}^{t_{i, j}} d_{i}\left(t, p_{i}\right) d t+\phi_{i, j}$, where $\phi_{i, j}$ is the observation error. The demand sequence for the $i$-th replenishment is given by $\boldsymbol{d}_{i}=\left[d_{i}\left(t_{i, 1}\right), d_{i}\left(t_{i, 2}\right), \cdots, d_{i}\left(t_{i, n_{i}}\right)\right]^{\top}$. Finally, the inventory data for the $i$-th replenishment are represented as $\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=\left\{Q_{i}, p_{i}, \boldsymbol{d}_{i}, \boldsymbol{l}_{i}\right\}$, and the historical inventory data from $m$ replenishment are $\boldsymbol{X}=\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}, \boldsymbol{X}_{2}, \cdots, \boldsymbol{X}_{m}\right\}$.

The inventory level of the $i$-th replenishment satisfies the differential equation:

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\mathrm{d} I_{i}(t)}{\mathrm{d} t}=-\lambda I_{i}(t)-d_{i}\left(t, p_{i}\right) \tag{7}
\end{equation*}
$$

with the initial condition $I\left(t_{i 0}\right)=Q_{i}$. Where, the demand rate is

$$
\begin{equation*}
d_{i}\left(t, p_{i}\right)=\left(\alpha-\beta p_{i}\right) e^{-\lambda\left(t-t_{i 0}\right)} \tag{8}
\end{equation*}
$$

The deterioration rate $\lambda$, the potential demand $\alpha$, and the price sensitivity coefficient $\beta$ need to be estimated, represented as $\boldsymbol{\Theta}=[\lambda, \alpha, \beta]^{\top}$.Accordingly, the goal of grey forecasting modelling is to identify the parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\Theta}$ from the inventory data $\boldsymbol{X} \boldsymbol{.}$

### 3.2. Problem transformation

Based on the sequence of inventory changes $\boldsymbol{l}_{i}$, the accumulated generation sequence $\boldsymbol{L}_{i}=\left[L_{i}\left(t_{i, 1}\right), L_{i}\left(t_{i, 2}\right), \cdots, L_{i}\left(t_{i, n_{i}}\right)\right]^{\top}$ is generated using the accumulated generating operator (AGO), defined as

$$
\begin{equation*}
L_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=Q_{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{j} \nabla t_{i, k} l_{i}\left(t_{i, k}\right), \nabla t_{i, k}=t_{i, k}-t_{i, k-1} \tag{9}
\end{equation*}
$$

for $j=1,2, \cdots, n_{i}$. Physically, the accumulated generation sequence $\boldsymbol{L}_{i}$ can be interpreted as observations of the inventory level, where $L_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=I_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)+\Psi_{i j}$, and $\Psi_{i j}$ denotes measurement noise.

Integrating both sides of Eq. (7) over the interval $\left[t_{i, j-1}, t_{i, j}\right]$ yields the discrete inventory equation:

$$
\begin{equation*}
\nabla I_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=-\lambda \int_{t_{i, j-1}}^{t_{i, j}} I_{i}(t) \mathrm{d} t-\int_{t_{i, j-1}}^{t_{i, j}} d_{i}\left(t, p_{i}\right) \mathrm{d} t \tag{10}
\end{equation*}
$$

The definite integral $\int_{t_{i, j-1}}^{t_{i, j}} I_{i}(t) \mathrm{d} t$ can be approximated using the neighbour mean generation operator of inventory level sequence $\boldsymbol{L}_{i}$, expressed as
$\int_{t_{i, j-1}}^{t_{i, j}} I_{i}(t) \mathrm{d} t \approx \frac{1}{2}\left[L\left(t_{i, j}\right)+L\left(t_{i, j-1}\right)\right] \nabla t_{i, j}$
where $\nabla t_{i, j}==t_{i, j}-t_{i, j-1}$. In addition, substituting the observed inventory change $\boldsymbol{l}_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)$ and the observed demand $d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)$ into Eq. (10) yields an inventory regression equation:

$$
\begin{equation*}
l_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=-\lambda \frac{1}{2}\left[L\left(t_{i, j}\right)+L\left(t_{i, j-1}\right)\right] \nabla t_{i, j}-d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)+\varphi_{i j} \tag{11}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\varphi_{i j}$ represents the model error associated with the approximation and measurement noise. Because the deterioration rate $\lambda$ only appears as a multiplicative coefficient of the known term, the inventory regression equation is clearly linear with respect to $\lambda$.

Similarly, by substituting the price $p_{i}$ and the observed demand sequence $\boldsymbol{d}_{i}$ into the demand observation equation
$d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=\int_{t_{i, j-1}}^{t_{i, j}} d_{i}\left(t, p_{i}\right) d t+\phi_{i, j}$ gives a demand regression equation:

$$
\begin{equation*}
d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\alpha-\beta p_{i}\right)\left[e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j-1}-t_{i 0}\right)}-e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j}-t_{i 0}\right)}\right]+\phi_{i, j} \tag{12}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\phi_{i, j}$ denotes the associated model error. The appearance of the exponential terms $e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j-1}-t_{i 0}\right)}$ and $e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j}-t_{i 0}\right)}$ clearly indicates that the demand regression equation is non-linear with respect to the deterioration rate $\lambda$.

By combining the two regression equations, we obtain the following joint regression model:

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
l_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=-\lambda \frac{1}{2}\left[L\left(t_{i, j}\right)+L\left(t_{i, j-1}\right)\right] \nabla t_{i, j}-d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)+\varphi_{i j}  \tag{13a}\\
d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\alpha-\beta p_{i}\right)\left[e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j-1}-t_{i 0}\right)}-e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j}-t_{i 0}\right)}\right]+\phi_{i, j}
\end{array}\right.
$$

for $j=1,2, \cdots, n_{i}, i=1,2, \cdots, m$. The error terms $\varphi_{i j}$ and $\phi_{i, j}$ are assumed to have zero mean, with variances $\sigma_{I}^{2}$ and $\sigma_{d}^{2}$, respectively. Although the inventory regression equation (13a) is linear in $\lambda$ (allowing a direct initial estimate of $\lambda$ ), the demand regression equation (13b) is nonlinear in $\lambda$ due to the presence of exponential terms. Additionally, because the observation errors $\psi_{i j}$ and $\Psi_{i j}$ are internally coupled, it is difficult to impose a specific probabilistic distribution on the model error $\varphi_{i j}$ in equation (13a). In such cases, the least squares framework remains advantageous, as it provides the best linear unbiased estimates even without knowledge of error distribution. For this reason, an iterative reweighed least-squares (IRLS) procedure is employed to jointly estimate all parameters with high accuracy.

### 3.3. Parameter estimation algorithm

Considering the different variances of $\varphi_{i j}$ and $\phi_{i, j}$, the weighted least squares loss for the joint regression model is

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
\mathscr{L}_{I}\left(\lambda ; \sigma_{I}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{I}^{2}} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[l_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)+d\left(t_{i, j}\right)+\lambda \frac{1}{2}\left[L\left(t_{i, j}\right)+L\left(t_{i, j-1}\right)\right] \nabla t_{i, j}\right]^{2}(14 \mathrm{a}) \\
\mathscr{L}_{d}\left(\Theta ; \sigma_{d}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{d}^{2}} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)-\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\alpha-\beta p_{i}\right)\left[e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j-1}-t_{i 0}\right)}-e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j}-t_{i 0}\right)}\right]\right]^{2}(14 \mathrm{~b})
\end{array}\right.
$$

For the loss function $\mathscr{L}_{d}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda} ; \sigma_{d}^{2}\right)$, by fixing $\lambda$, the least squares estimator of $\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\lambda}=[\alpha, \beta]^{\top}$ is given by

$$
\begin{equation*}
\widehat{\Xi}_{\lambda}=\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}^{\top} \boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}\right)^{-1} \boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}^{\top} \boldsymbol{Y} \tag{15}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{Y}=\operatorname{vec}(\boldsymbol{D}), \boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}=[\operatorname{vec}(\boldsymbol{E}), \operatorname{vec}(\boldsymbol{P})]$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{D} & =\left[\begin{array}{c}
d_{1}\left(t_{1,1}\right), d_{2}\left(t_{2,1}\right), \cdots, d_{m}\left(t_{m, 1}\right) \\
d_{1}\left(t_{1,2}\right), d_{2}\left(t_{2,2}\right), \cdots, d_{m}\left(t_{m, 2}\right) \\
\cdots \\
d_{1}\left(t_{1, n_{1}}\right), d_{2}\left(t_{2, n_{2}}\right), \cdots, d_{m}\left(t_{m, n_{m}}\right)
\end{array}\right], \boldsymbol{E}=\left[\begin{array}{c}
E_{1,1}, E_{2,1}, \cdots, E_{m, 1} \\
E_{1,2}, E_{2,2}, \cdots, E_{m, 2} \\
\cdots \\
E_{1, n_{1}}, E_{2, n_{2}}, \cdots, E_{m, n_{m}}
\end{array}\right] \\
\boldsymbol{P} & =\left[\begin{array}{c}
P_{1,1}, P_{2,1}, \cdots, P_{m, 1} \\
P_{1,2}, P_{2,2}, \cdots, P_{m, 2} \\
\cdots \\
P_{1, n_{1}}, P_{2, n_{2}}, \cdots, P_{m, n_{m}}
\end{array}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

with $E_{i, j}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j-1}-t_{i 0}\right)}-e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j}-t_{i 0}\right)}\right], P_{i, j}=-\frac{1}{\lambda} p_{i}\left[e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j-1}-t_{i 0}\right)}-e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j}-t_{i 0}\right)}\right]=-p_{i} E_{i, j}$. By substituting $\Xi_{\lambda}$ into (14b), the loss function $\mathscr{L}_{d}\left(\Theta ; \sigma_{d}^{2}\right)$ is reduced to $\mathscr{L}_{d}\left(\lambda ; \sigma_{d}^{2}\right)$. In this form, the loss function depends solely on the deterioration rate $\lambda$, with all other parameters effectively eliminated.

By combining the loss functions (14a) and (14b), the composite loss function is obtained as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\mathscr{L}\left(\lambda ; \sigma_{I}^{2}, \sigma_{d}^{2}\right)=\mathscr{L}_{I}\left(\lambda ; \sigma_{I}^{2}\right)+\mathscr{L}_{d}\left(\lambda ; \sigma_{d}^{2}\right) \tag{16}
\end{equation*}
$$

The composite loss function depends on the parameter $\lambda$ as well as the variances $\sigma_{I}^{2}, \sigma_{d}^{2}$. If $\sigma_{I}^{2}$ and $\sigma_{d}^{2}$ are known, $\lambda$ could be obtained by non-linear least squares. Conversely, if $\lambda$ are known, Eqs. (13a) and (13b) would deliver $\sigma_{I}^{2}$ and $\sigma_{d}^{2}$. Because the parameter $\lambda$ and variances $\sigma_{I}^{2}, \sigma_{d}^{2}$ are mutually dependent, the iterative reweighed least squares algorithm alternates between updating the variance estimates and refining the estimate of $\lambda$, which first estimates the two variances, $\sigma_{I}^{2}$ and $\sigma_{d}^{2}$, and then reestimates $\lambda$ based on the updated variance estimates. The IRLS procedure proceeds as follows: (i) initialize $\lambda$ with an initial value; (ii) estimate the variances $\sigma_{I}^{2}$ and $\sigma_{d}^{2}$; (iii) re-estimate $\lambda$ using the updated variances; (iv) repeat steps (ii)-(iii) until convergence.

Because the inventory regression Eq. (11) is linear in $\lambda$ and all required data are observable, an initial estimate of $\lambda$ can be obtained directly from this equation. This preliminary estimate serves as the starting point for the IRLS algorithm, which iteratively refines the value of $\lambda$. During each IRLS iteration, once the variances $\sigma_{I}^{2}$ and $\sigma_{d}^{2}$ have been updated, $\lambda$ is re-estimated using the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm, provided by MATLAB function Isqnonlin (MATLAB, 2006). Through this iterative procedure, the parameters of the differential equation (7) are determined. As a result, the inventory dynamics described by Eq. (2) become fully specified. Building on this identified model, the next section formulates an optimization framework for the inventory system.

## 4. Inventory optimization model for deteriorating products

Building on the differential equation from Section 3, this section develops an optimization model to determine the inventory decisions. The operation of the inventory system involves three types of costs: fixed ordering cost, procurement cost, and holding cost. The fixed ordering cost $K(K>0)$ denotes the constant expense incurred with each order, independent of the order quantity. The procurement cost depends on the unit production cost $c(c>0)$ and the order quantity, reflecting the direct expenditure required for each replenishment. The holding cost $h(h>0)$ accounts for expenses associated with storage, including warehouse fees, insurance, and other related charges.

By summing these three cost, the total cost over an entire cycle is obtained as

$$
\begin{align*}
C & =K+c Q+h \int_{t_{0}}^{t_{T}} I(\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau  \tag{17}\\
& =K+c(\alpha-\beta p) T+\frac{h(\alpha-\beta p) T}{\lambda}+\frac{h(\alpha-\beta p)}{\lambda^{2}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}-1\right]
\end{align*}
$$

Denoting the selling price as $p$, the total revenue over the entire cycle is

$$
\begin{equation*}
R=p \int_{t_{0}}^{t_{T}} D(\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau=\frac{p(\alpha-\beta p)}{\lambda}\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}\right] \tag{18}
\end{equation*}
$$

The profit per unit time is expressed as $P=(R-C) / T$, which can be further simplified to

$$
\begin{equation*}
P(p, T)=\frac{(\alpha-\beta p)(p \lambda+h)}{\lambda^{2} T}\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}\right]-\frac{(\alpha-\beta p)(c \lambda+h)}{\lambda}-K / T \tag{19}
\end{equation*}
$$

With price and ordering cycle as optimization variables, the inventory optimization model aims to maximize the profit function $P(p, T)$.

Let the partial derivatives of $P(p, T)$ with respect to $p$ and $T$ be 0 . Then, the necessary conditions for maximizing $P(p, T)$ can be expressed as

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
\frac{\partial P(p, T)}{\partial p}=\frac{(\alpha-\beta p) \lambda-b(p \lambda+h)}{\lambda^{2} T}\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}\right]-\frac{(\alpha-\beta p) c-\beta(c \lambda+h)}{\lambda}=0  \tag{20}\\
\frac{\partial P(p, T)}{\partial T}=\frac{(\alpha-\beta p)(p \lambda+h) T \lambda \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}-(\alpha-\beta p)(p \lambda+h)\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}-1\right)-K \lambda^{2}}{\lambda^{2} T^{2}}=0
\end{array}\right.
$$

The optimal price $p^{*}$ and the optimal ordering cycle $T^{*}$ can be obtained by numerically solving Eq. (20). Considering the complexity of solving Eq. (20), the profit in Eq. (19) can be approximated using Taylor's expansion.

According to the Taylor expansion, for small $\lambda T$, the exponential term $\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}$ in profit expression (19) can be approximated by the quadratic polynomial $1-\lambda T+\lambda^{2} T^{2} / 2$, with an error on the order of $\mathscr{O}\left(\lambda^{3} T^{3}\right)$. In practice, the deterioration rates for fresh products are typically below 0.05 , and ordering cycles are generally shorter than 4 days, yielding $\lambda T<0.1$. Under these conditions, the quadratic approximation is sufficiently accurate for analytical and computational purposes.

Using this approximation, the average profit per unit time can be simplified as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\widetilde{P}(p, T)=(\alpha-\beta p)(p-c)-(\alpha-\beta p)(p \lambda+h) T / 2-K / T \tag{21}
\end{equation*}
$$

Setting the first derivative of $\widetilde{P}(p, T)$ with respect to $T$ equal to zero gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial \widetilde{P}(p, T)}{\partial T}=-(\alpha-\beta p)(p \lambda+h) / 2+K / T^{2}=0 \tag{22}
\end{equation*}
$$

Solving Eq. (22) yields

$$
\begin{equation*}
\widetilde{T}_{p}^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{2 K}{(p \lambda+h)(\alpha-\beta p)}} \tag{23}
\end{equation*}
$$

For a fixed selling price $p$, the optimal replenishment cycle increases with the ordering cost $K$ but decreases with the deterioration rate $\lambda$ and the holding cost $h$.

Substituting $\widetilde{T}_{p}^{*}$ into the approximate average profit $\widetilde{P}(p, T)$, we obtain

$$
\begin{equation*}
\widetilde{P}\left(p, \widetilde{T}_{p}^{*}\right)=(p-c)(\alpha-\beta p)-\sqrt{2 K(p \lambda+h)(\alpha-\beta p)} \tag{24}
\end{equation*}
$$

Setting the first-order derivative of $\widetilde{P}\left(p, \widetilde{T}_{p}^{*}\right)$ with respect to $p$ equal to zero yields

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial \widetilde{P}\left(p, \widetilde{T}_{p}^{*}\right)}{\partial p}=(\alpha-\beta p)-\beta\left[p-c-\sqrt{\frac{K[p \lambda+h]}{2(\alpha-\beta p)}}=0\right. \tag{25}
\end{equation*}
$$

The optimal price $\tilde{p}^{*}$ can be obtained by numerically solving Eq. (25) using MATLAB. Since $\widetilde{P}\left(c, \widetilde{T}_{p}^{*}\right)= -\sqrt{2 K(p \lambda+h)(\alpha-\beta c)}<0, \widetilde{P}\left(\alpha / \beta, \widetilde{T}_{p}^{*}\right)=0$, and $\left.\frac{\partial \widetilde{P}\left(p, \widetilde{T}_{p}^{*}\right)}{\partial p}\right|_{p=c}=(\alpha-\beta c)+\beta \sqrt{\frac{K[p \lambda+h]}{2(\alpha-\beta c)}}>0$, it follows that $\widetilde{P}\left(p, \widetilde{T}_{p}^{*}\right)$ has local maximum satisfying condition (25) within the interval $[c, \alpha / \beta]$. The optimal order quantity and maximum profit are given by

$$
\begin{equation*}
\widetilde{Q}^{*}=I\left(t_{0}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2 K\left(\alpha-\beta \tilde{p}^{*}\right)}{p \lambda+h}} \tag{26}
\end{equation*}
$$

and

$$
\begin{equation*}
\widetilde{P}\left(\tilde{p}^{*}, \widetilde{T}_{p}^{*}\right)=\left(\tilde{p}^{*}-c\right)\left(\alpha-\beta \tilde{p}^{*}\right)-\sqrt{2 K\left(\alpha-\beta \tilde{p}^{*}\right)\left(\tilde{p}^{*} \lambda+h\right)} \tag{27}
\end{equation*}
$$

This section presents the inventory optimization model and its solution approach. In cases where the profit function is not easily solvable, an approximate profit function is introduced to simplify the solution procedure.

## 5. Simulation experiment

To verify the feasibility of the grey forecasting model, this section introduces an inventory simulation approach to generate simulation inventory data. A typical simulation approach involves incorporating random noise into the solution of the inventory differential equation. However, since inventory levels typically exhibit a monotonically decreasing trend within each ordering cycle, introducing such noise may disrupt this inherent property. To overcome this challenge, we derive the discrete form of the differential equation and simulate inventory reductions using a Poisson distribution.

### 5.1. Simulation settings

First, the demand simulation equation is obtained from the demand regression Eq. (12):

$$
\begin{equation*}
d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\alpha-\beta p_{i}\right)\left[e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j-1}-t_{i 0}\right)}-e^{-\lambda\left(t_{i, j}-t_{i 0}\right)}\right]+\phi_{i, j} \tag{28}
\end{equation*}
$$

Here, $\phi_{i, j}$ is assumed to follows a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance $\sigma_{d}^{2}$.
According to Eq. (10), the differential equation (7) can be discretized as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\nabla I_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=-\lambda \frac{1}{2}\left[I\left(t_{i, j}\right)+I\left(t_{i, j-1}\right)\right] \nabla t_{i, j}-d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right) \tag{29}
\end{equation*}
$$

Let $\psi_{i, j}$ be a random variable following a Poisson distribution with a mean of $\lambda \frac{1}{2}\left[I\left(t_{i, j}\right)+I\left(t_{i, j-1}\right)\right] \nabla t_{i, j}$. The inventory changes can then be simulated recursively as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\nabla I_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right)=-\psi_{i, j}-d_{i}\left(t_{i, j}\right) \tag{30}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $I_{i}\left(t_{i, 0}\right)=Q_{i}$.
In Eqs. (27) and (28), the quantity decay rate $\lambda$ is assigned to 0.05 , while the potential demand $\alpha$ and the price sensitivity coefficient $\beta$ in (27) are set to 120 and 10 , respectively. In Eq. (27), the price $p_{i}$ is randomly drawn from a uniform distribution over the interval [5, 8], and the variance is specify as $\sigma_{d}^{2}=25$. In Eq. (28), the order quantity $Q_{i}$ is randomly sampled from a uniform integer distribution over the range [360,480], with a data collection interval of $\nabla t_{i, j}=1$. For the $i$-th order cycle, the inventory data are defined as $\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=\left\{Q_{i}, p_{i}, \boldsymbol{d}_{i}, \boldsymbol{l}_{i}\right\}$, and the complete dataset for 10 ordering cycles is represented as $\boldsymbol{X}=\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}, \boldsymbol{X}_{2}, \cdots, \boldsymbol{X}_{10}\right\}$.

## 6. Conclusion

To tackle the challenges of establishing inventory models for deteriorating products in real-world scenarios, this study introduces a grey forecasting approach to identify the inventory differential equation from inventory data and subsequently establishes an inventory optimization model. The key contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

1. An inventory system with interdependent quality and quantity decay is developed. The quality level is defined as the proportion of unspoiled items, linking quality decay with quantity decay. Given the combined effect of price and quality on demand, the demand rate is modelled as a function of both quality and price. A differential equation is then formulated to describe inventory levels based on these factors.
2. A grey forecasting modelling approach for deteriorating inventory is proposed. By applying the accumulated generating operator to uncover unobserved information, the fitting problem of the inventory differential equation is transformed into a joint regression problem, which is then solved using the iteratively reweighed least squares algorithm.
3. The process and steps of grey forecasting modelling and inventory optimization are demonstrated through a numerical experiment. Simulation results confirm the feasibility of using grey forecasting modelling in inventory management.

For the first time, this paper applies grey forecasting modelling to inventory management, presents a integrated modelling framework for generating operational decisions from inventory data. Grey forecasting modelling is employed to uncover inventory differential equations, which are then incorporated into the inventory optimization model to generate inventory decisions. The integrated framework can provide data-supported decision-making solutions for the practical management of deteriorating products. In addition, it also presents a comprehensive case study of grey forecasting modelling, offering practical insights for improving the grey forecasting model.

From a practical perspective, this paper initially validates the feasibility of the grey forecasting modelling approach through a simulation case. By reducing reliance on human expertise, the proposed approach can assist retailers develop automated expert systems for inventory decision-making. Future research will focus on analyzing the inventory system using real-world datasets, developing a tailored grey forecasting modelling approach, and providing intelligent decision support for inventory management.
